## The story

A young man named Werther leaves the city and settles in a small country town, and everything we know of him arrives in the letters he writes to his friend Wilhelm - my Spanish edition is an old translation that turns him into Guillermo, and Lotte into Carlota, which I ended up finding charming. At a country dance he meets Lotte, a young woman raising her many little siblings after their mother's death, and he falls for her completely, in the total way he does everything. She is engaged to Albert, a kind and sensible man. Werther knows it from the start, and he stays anyway. What follows is probably the most famous ending in European literature, so I will not tiptoe around it: the book closes with Werther's suicide.

## The debate of the 12th of August

If I had to keep one scene, it is this one. Werther visits Albert, picks up one of his pistols, and the talk drifts into suicide. Albert says what most sensible people said then and say now: that it is weakness, that it is easier to die than to bear a painful life with courage. Werther answers with an analogy that has not aged a day: nobody calls a man a coward for dying of a fever, so why call him one when the sickness is in the soul? Human nature has its limits, he says; suffering past a certain point destroys a person exactly the way an illness does.

I side with Werther here, and not because the book asks me to. You cannot judge what you have not lived. Every person is a world, and there is no instrument for measuring someone else's suffering; Albert's tidy verdict is delivered from a settled life, by a man who has never had to test his own theory. But the scene has a second floor that I only saw afterwards: neither of them is really arguing philosophy. Each one, without knowing it, is describing his own destiny - Albert the resolved life he will go on living, Werther the door he will eventually use. Three months earlier, in the letter of the 22nd of May, he had already written it down, talking about the man who knows

> that he can leave this prison whenever he so decides.

The permission was signed long before the debate. The conversation with Albert only read it aloud. And the pistols are the same ones: at the end of the book Werther borrows them from Albert with the excuse of a journey, and it is Lotte's own hands that give them over.

## A being of light

On my shelf this book sits next to *No Longer Human*, *The Stranger* and *Notes from the Underground*, and it belongs there: another young man who does not fit the world he was given. But the resemblance ends at the door, because those narrators are sombre, and Werther is almost a being of light. He writes like a poet even when he is only telling Wilhelm about his day. He drinks the landscape, adores children, finds "a whole world in himself", and describes the energy that nature transmits to him with a passion that none of those other books contain. Reading Dazai you watch a man hide from life; reading Werther you watch a man love it too much to accept it in halves.

The letters I keep going back to are from that early, luminous stretch. On the 22nd of May he looks at respectable adults and sees big children, stumbling through the world without knowing where they come from or where they are going, led by sweets and punishments, and nobody wants to admit it. On the 26th he takes on rules themselves: a man formed by rules will never produce anything senseless or bad, he concedes, but never anything remarkable either, because every rule prunes the shoots before they can grow. He has a point, and so does the other side: conventions limit the individual, and they are also what lets us live together at all. It is a debate as old as living in towns, and the book states it better than most philosophy does.

## The same flame

Here is what I ended up believing about Werther: his virtue and his flaw are the same thing. What makes him luminous - feeling everything without a filter, at full volume - is exactly what destroys him. Wilhelm warns him more than once to clear his head and stop chasing a woman who is already promised. Werther even half-obeys: he takes the diplomatic post he had been postponing until he had no other choice, lasts a while, and comes straight back to the couple. A more practical man would have stayed away; a more practical man, in some other life, might even have been the one standing beside Lotte instead of Albert, since it is each man's attitudes, more than any fate, that decide who ends up where. But a more practical Werther would never have written these letters. You cannot feel a sunset that hard and expect to feel a rejection softly. I cannot wish the intensity away without losing the poet, and neither could he.

## Lotte's part

Was it love? Partly, I think, and partly an obsession he built himself. Everyone around him says the obvious thing - the world is full of people still to meet, someone else will set him alight the same way - and near the end even Lotte says it to him. He cannot hear it, because the obsession has long since stopped being about her and become the room he lives in. And yet honesty requires one more sentence: Lotte is not weightless in this story. She was engaged from the first day, she saw perfectly well what was happening to him, and she did not truly push him away until practically the end. I do not blame her - she was young, and being adored is warm - but the tragedy has more than one author.

## The editor steps in

The last stretch of the book does something I had never seen before. The letters stop, and a voice calling itself the editor takes over, reconstructing Werther's final weeks from notes and testimony. When I saw the heading I honestly thought for a moment that the actual publisher was talking. Then you realise it is part of the work itself, and the change of temperature is the point: after two hundred pages inside Werther's head, someone describes him coldly from outside, down to the workmen carrying the coffin and the absence of any priest. After all that fire, the book ends in plain administrative frost, and it is devastating.

## The fever that left the book

What I did not know while reading is what this little book did to the world. Goethe wrote it in 1774, at twenty-four, in a matter of weeks, out of his own hopeless love for a woman engaged to his friend — writing it was his exit, the door he opened instead of Werther's. The novel became Europe's first modern bestseller: young men dressed in Werther's blue coat and yellow waistcoat, there was Werther merchandise and Werther pilgrimages, and Napoleon told Goethe he had read it seven times and carried it on his campaign in Egypt. And there was a darker echo: reports of young readers taking their own lives with the book nearby, enough for it to be banned in Leipzig, Copenhagen and Italy. Two centuries later, in 1974, the sociologist David Phillips proved statistically that widely publicised suicides produce imitations, and he named the phenomenon the Werther effect. It is the reason newspapers today follow strict guidelines when they report a suicide. A stack of letters from 1774 ended up founding a concept modern sociology still uses, which is more afterlife than almost any novel gets.

## Final thoughts

The reason this book has not aged is that it walks through stations almost everyone passes: the first love that feels like the only love that will ever exist, the advice of friends you cannot hear, the debate between what the soul wants and what life demands. I could not help feeling recognised in Werther's obsession. All of us have loved someone that way once(whether it was returned or not is nobody's business) and the lesson I take is to learn what Werther could not: that kind of total surrender does more damage than we imagine, and there is always more life, and more people, than the one person we cannot have. His mistake was never the loving; it was refusing every exit that life kept politely offering him.

I read it in a single day, a stretch in the morning and another in the afternoon, and it never once felt like homework. The people I would press it on are the ones who have just arrived at adulthood, because that is the only age at which you read it from the inside, with Werther. Ten years later you will read it from Albert's chair, or Wilhelm's, and it will be a different book, probably a wiser one, certainly a colder one. Both readings are worth having, but the first is only possible once, and it is the one Goethe wrote the book for.
