## The story

A young woman named Miyu lives alone in the city, and her cat, Chobi, tells us about her days: the commute, the empty flat, the small weather of an ordinary life. From that little seed the book grows four linked stories, four women and four cats, each chapter alternating between the human voice and the feline one. There is Reina, who cannot work up the courage to sit her fine-arts entrance exams, and her cat Mimi; there is Aoi, who has shut herself away after the death of her best friend, and Cookie, the cat who ends up in her hands; and there is Shino, in the quietest season of her life, with her old black cat Kuro. The lives brush against one another as the seasons turn, mostly without the women ever knowing, and the cats are the ones carrying the connections.

## The scene at the door

There is a scene in which Aoi decides to leave the house. She has been shut inside for a long while by then, caught between the desire to return to the world and the fear of losing anyone else, and her reasons are the heaviest in the book. Nobody argues her out of that room. There is no friend with the right words, no speech, no revelation. What finally gets her out is Cookie, doing the only things a cat knows how to do.

It is the best page in the book. And everything I want to say about the book, the thing it does brilliantly and the one thing it struggles with, comes out of that single page.

## A five-minute film, and a whole book to fill

I knew Makoto Shinkai's films already, which is the only reason this book caught my eye on a bookshop table, one day when a friend and I were killing time before lunch. What I did not know then, and the cover does not make obvious, is where it actually comes from. *She and Her Cat* is not a novel by Shinkai: it is a novelisation, written by Naruki Nagakawa from Shinkai's material, and the original is a five-minute short film from 1999, in which a cat narrates his life with the girl who took him in. That is the whole inheritance. Five minutes of voice-over and afternoon light.

So Nagakawa's problem was not artistic but arithmetic: how do you turn five minutes into a book? His answer was to multiply the structure by four. Keep Miyu and Chobi, then add three more women, three more cats, three more wounds, and let the chapters pass the voice back and forth between owner and animal. The entire novel stands or falls on that one decision, and the curious thing is that it does both at once.

## The cat as the camera

If you know *5 Centimeters per Second* or *The Garden of Words*, you know Shinkai's method: the characters never say what they feel. The framing says it, the light says it, a passing train says it. He has spent over twenty years making films about people who cannot put themselves into words.

Prose has no camera, and the real discovery of this novel is what it puts in the camera's place: the cat. The cat sees what the woman cannot say and says it for her, plainly, without analysis, and without any character having to deliver the explanatory speech that would ruin the scene. That is why the page at Aoi's door works. Nobody explains to Aoi what is happening to her; it is shown to her by an animal that does not even know it is showing anything. This is narrative economy of the first order, and it is what lifts the book above the shelf of comforting Japanese fiction where it usually sits.

## Fifty pages per wound

But the same arithmetic has a cost. Four stories in two hundred pages works out to roughly fifty pages each: fifty pages to set up a wound, bring in a cat, and close. It is not quite enough, and so each wound has to arrive already formed.

You feel it most with Aoi herself. Her friend's death, the engine of her whole chapter, appears practically out of nowhere: no build-up, no accumulated weight, just a fact the text needs you to accept so that the later scene can move you. Compare that with what Shinkai does on film, where a single separation can fill the entire movie. Here, grief is a premise rather than a process.

And there is a gentler objection, one that is harder to voice: the cat protects the book a little. If you point out that a kitten cannot simply unlock a person paralysed by guilt, you feel as though you are arguing with the kitten itself. I say this with real affection, because I did like the book. But the comfort it offers arrives, at times, a little before it has been earned.

## The flaw and the virtue

The two are the same mechanism, though. The compression that short-changes Aoi's grief is what makes the scene at her door shine. Fifty pages leave no room to explain anything, and so the story cannot talk its way to the ending it wants.

> With no room left to explain, the story can only act, and acting is the only thing a cat knows how to do.

A cat cannot deliver a speech about grief, cannot reason you back into the world, cannot promise that everything will be all right. It can only scratch at the door and wait for you on the other side of it. The book's weakness and the book's best page are one thing, seen from its two sides, and there is no version of the book that has one without the other.

## Final thoughts

For all my nitpicking, this is not a sad book, and it never felt heavy. What it left me with is simpler: the understanding that sometimes the best companion is not a human one. The four stories quietly answer each other, a cat slipping into a story where the humans never quite meet, and that thread is a real pleasure to follow. The whole thing bends, always, towards comfort.

That is also the honest way to recommend it. Give it to anyone who has seen Shinkai's films and wants to watch his method working without a single image to hold it up, to anyone who loves cats, and to anyone who wants a short, warm book for a single day. Just know what you are picking up: nothing here resists you; everything consoles.

As for me: I am allergic to cats. I love them, my friends have them, and if I fuss over one too long my eyes start to itch. I will probably never live with one. A book like this is about the closest I will get to sharing a room with a cat, and for one afternoon, it was more than enough.
