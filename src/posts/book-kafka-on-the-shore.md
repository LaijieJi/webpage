## The story

Two stories run alongside each other, chapter by chapter, and for a long while you have no idea what they have to do with one another. In the first, a fifteen-year-old boy who calls himself Kafka Tamura runs away from his father's house in Tokyo, chased by a dark prophecy and drawn toward a little library in a distant town. In the second, an old man named Nakata, who cannot read a single word but can hold a conversation with any cat, sets off on a journey he does not understand, following a logic entirely his own. Haruki Murakami lets the two paths bend slowly toward each other, and toward something far stranger than either of them could guess.

## My first Murakami

*Kafka on the Shore* was my first real step into Japanese literature, and into Murakami, and from the opening pages I could feel I was somewhere new. The style was unlike anything I had read before, calm and matter-of-fact even when the events it describes are anything but. Murakami will narrate a boy fleeing a curse and an old man chatting with a cat in exactly the same steady, unhurried voice, and somehow that evenness is what makes the strangeness go down so easily.

What pulled me in straight away was the way it begins. Kafka slips out of his home and into the world almost as though in a trance, urged on by a presence he calls the boy named Crow, a kind of imaginary friend, or second self, who speaks to him whenever he needs steadying. Right from the start you are told, very quietly, that the ordinary rules are not entirely in force here, and that you should probably stop expecting them to be. And then the book splits in two, alternating between Kafka and Nakata, two lives that could not seem further apart, trusting you to hold both until it is ready to show you the thread running between them.

## Nakata, and the art of accepting your fate

If Kafka is the heart of the book, Nakata is its soul. An old man left simple after a strange incident in his childhood, he moves through the world with a gentleness that stopped me every time he appeared. He does not rage against what happened to him, nor mourn the life he might otherwise have had. He simply accepts what fate has handed him and lives it fully, talking to cats, eating his eel, resting when he is tired, and finding a genuine happiness in all of it.

My favourite thing in the whole book is Nakata and the cats. At first it is simply funny, an old man gravely interviewing the neighbourhood cats about one that has gone missing. But somewhere along the way it stops being a joke and starts to ache a little, because you realise the cats are not a quirk, they are his company. Nakata has almost no one, and it is plainly easier for him to sit and feel at home beside a cat than beside any person. Those conversations are where he is most himself, most gently understood, and Murakami lets them carry a tenderness I never saw coming from what had looked, at first, like a gag.

And there is a small reward in it, because the man who connects more easily with cats than with people does, in the end, find a human companion of his own, and the whole thread he belongs to closes on a note of real peace.

## Oshima, and the ones who keep you moving

Kafka does not survive his own story alone, and the person I came to value most along the way was Oshima, the librarian who takes him in. Oshima does far more than hide a runaway boy. He gives Kafka a safe place to be, a room, a refuge, a sheltered corner of the world where he is allowed to simply exist for a while, and then, just as importantly, keeps him moving forward when it would be far easier to stop. He is sharp, kind, and almost without judgement, the sort of steady presence that lets a frightened kid keep going. In a book so full of the uncanny, Oshima is something rarer still: ordinary, decent, human care, offered without a price.

## Johnnie Walker, Colonel Sanders, and the turn into the strange

And then the book simply refuses to behave. I still remember the jolt of first hearing the name Johnnie Walker turn up as an actual character, and, later, a man who looks and talks exactly like Colonel Sanders strolling in to hand out cryptic advice. Taking those familiar labels and pinning them onto figures out of some other reality made everything tilt, in the best possible way. It was so unexpected that it pushed the whole story into a stranger, headier place, and I loved it for that.

Johnnie Walker is not only strange, though, he is the hinge. It is his cruelty that finally forces Nakata to snap, to do the one violent, decisive thing that turns his small, settled life into the journey the rest of the book follows. From there the impossible keeps arriving as though it were nothing at all: fish fall from the sky, a stone becomes a doorway, and Kafka finds his way into a hidden world deep in the forest, where time does not run the way it should, and where the ending waiting for him, and for Miss Saeki, was nothing I could ever have predicted. Almost none of it is explained, and to me that felt exactly right, just enough mystery and not a drop more. It is the kind of book that leaves you wanting to grab someone by the arm and tell them the whole thing, simply because it is such a breath of fresh air, so new and so unexpected.

## The prophecy, and how far it goes

Underneath the wonder, though, runs a darker current, and it is worth being honest about it. Kafka's curse is an old and heavy one, Oedipal to its core: the prophecy that he will harm his father and end up with his mother and his sister. Murakami does not treat that as a distant, tidy symbol. He follows it, and he does not look away when it arrives.

This was the part that unsettled me, especially early on. Murakami does not hold back in how explicitly he writes the sexual side of the prophecy, and I had genuinely never read anything with that level of detail before. More than once I caught myself unsure whether it all truly needed to be quite so explicit, whether the frankness was serving the story or simply testing me. I still do not have a clean answer. It is part of what makes the book brave, and also part of what makes it, in places, genuinely uncomfortable to sit with.

## The magic in the little things

What I loved most, though, lives in the small things. Murakami pays an almost impossible amount of attention to the details most writers skip straight past: what a character cooks and eats, the record that happens to be playing, the exact feel of a room at a certain hour, the car someone drives. Nothing is too minor for him to notice, and it is precisely this patient, loving attention to the ordinary that makes everything else possible.

> He never asks you to believe in the impossible. He simply describes it so carefully that you already do.

That, in the end, is what I most want to highlight. Murakami takes a story rooted in a thoroughly real world and threads true magic all the way through it, and to me it lands closer to fantasy than to what people usually file under magic realism. And yet, resting on all those small, true details, the magic never once feels false. It feels like something that could be happening quietly, somewhere just out of sight.

## Final thoughts

*Kafka on the Shore* was the perfect first door into Murakami, strange and gentle and completely its own thing. It asks you to let go of needing everything to add up, and rewards you with a world that feels more alive than most realistic ones, cats and prophecies and falling fish and all. I closed it a little changed, and a great deal more curious.

It also turned out to be a beginning. This was the book that opened Murakami for me, and I went straight on to *Norwegian Wood* and *South of the Border, West of the Sun*, and loved them both, though in a completely different, quieter key. Of the two, *Norwegian Wood* was the one that truly stayed with me; *South of the Border* felt as though it were circling many of the same themes, but *Norwegian Wood* is where they finally bloomed. If you have never read Murakami, and you are willing to follow a story that trusts you to sit with a mystery rather than solve it, this is exactly where I would start.

## A side note from the garage

*Written a good while after I first read this, once I finally had a car of my own.*

One small detail has grown on me far more than it had any right to. Oshima drives a little Mazda roadster, and on my first read I slid straight past it as background colour. Since a Miata of my own found its way into the garage, a more modern version of that very same little car, those driving scenes read completely differently. I can suddenly recall exactly how Murakami describes the feel of being behind the wheel, the smallness of the thing, the closeness of the road, and it turns out he got it just right. It is a funny kind of magic of its own, a book reaching forward to meet a version of you who did not exist yet when you read it.
